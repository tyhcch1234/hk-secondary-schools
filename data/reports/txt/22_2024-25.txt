(1) Our School

Our Mission
⮚   Our mission is to educate our students to adopt a respectful and humble disposition in life and pursue
    endeavours with perseverance and earnestness, to become self-initiated learners, responsible citizens
    and tomorrow’s leaders with integrity. Our school motto is, “Reverence ( 敬 ), Humility ( 遜 ),
    Perseverance (時) and Earnestness (敏)”. On this basis, we educate our students to understand and
    accept themselves and others, to live virtuously, to have a life-long pursuit of knowledge for their
    personal growth and to possess social awareness and a sense of conscientiousness for the common
    good.

School Sponsoring Body
⮚   Po On Commercial Association was founded in 1914. With a heart set on education, it established in
    Hong Kong the Po On Commercial Association Wong Siu Ching Secondary School and the Po On
    Commercial Association Wan Ho Kan Primary School in 1979 and 1997 respectively. The Association
    has also sponsored various primary school renovation projects through Sowers Action in Hunan and
    Yunnan with a keen concern for alleviating poverty and promoting educational development in China.

Incorporated Management Committee
⮚   The Incorporated Management Committee (IMC) replaced the School Management Committee, the
    former governing body on 1 December 2011 to allow different stakeholders to be represented in school
    management and policy-making. Our IMC consists of 15 registered school managers, including 8
    Sponsoring Body Managers, the Principal, 2 Teacher Managers, 2 Parent Managers, 1 Alumni
    Manager and 1 Independent Manager. The IMC exercises supervision, offers guidance to the school
    and oversees the school’s daily operations.

Medium of Instruction
⮚   Starting from Form One in 2010/11, English has become the medium of instruction in teaching and
    examination for the following subjects: English Language, Mathematics, Science, Physics, Chemistry,
    Biology, History, Geography, Computer & Information Technology, Economics and Business,
    Accounting & Financial Studies. Chinese remains the medium of instruction for Chinese Language,
    Citizenship & Social Development, Citizenship, Economics & Society, Life & Society, Chinese
    History, Putonghua, Visual Arts, Physical Education, Music, and Design & Technology.

School Facilities
⮚   Our school is an aided, whole-day co-educational secondary school established in 1979. With a campus
    site area of over 6,000 m2, we are one of the largest schools in Tsuen Wan District, providing spacious
    areas for student activities.
⮚   The school has modern facilities and equipment, including 24 classrooms, 4 science laboratories, an
    outdoor playground, a covered playground, an assembly hall, an education research room, a library, a
    multi-media learning centre, a language laboratory, a Chinese room, an English room, a Mathematics
    room, a Geography room, a Design and Technology room, a History & self-directed learning room, a
    Visual art room, a Music room, 2 computer rooms and a counselling centre.
⮚   Each classroom is installed with a smart board, a computer, a visualizer and a projection system. The
    school hall, all classrooms and special rooms are air-conditioned.

Major Concern 1: To promote students' reading.

Achievements

 According to APASO survey data, approximately 68% of junior students and 65.5% of
    senior students showed a love for reading (P-values of 68 and 65.5, respectively). Around
    72.6% of junior students and 21.2% of senior students reported dedicating time to leisure
    reading. Over 85% of both junior and senior students demonstrated good reading strategies
    (Q-values of 116 for juniors and 115 for seniors), indicating strong capabilities in applying
    effective reading techniques.
 In the current academic year, subjects such as Chinese, English, Biology, and History
    collaborated with the library to encourage students to read. During professional development
    sessions, teachers incorporated reading elements into subjects like Mathematics and Science,
    such as using reading materials in pre-lesson tasks or assessments.

Reflection

 When assessing non-assigned reading, juniors scored a Q-value of 92 (P=29.7%), while
    seniors scored 85 (P=15.9%), suggesting room for improvement in voluntary reading habits.
    Factors influencing this may include competing demands on time, less appealing materials
    (noted by 45% of teachers for juniors and 40% for seniors), and limited student choice in
    reading selections. Both teachers and students can further strengthen the use of reading as a
    core learning tool, with a focus on integrating it more deeply into daily routines in the coming
    year.
 Collaboration with QSiP for Chinese and English subjects has continued, emphasizing
    reading strategies during lunchtime reading periods and in the primary-to-secondary
    transition program. The APASO results show high Q-values for good reading strategies (116
    for juniors, 115 for seniors), confirming students' acquisition of these skills. It is
    recommended to extend this collaboration next year to enhance cross-curricular reading,
    offering more opportunities for strategy application across subjects.

Feedback and Follow-up

 Reading strategy training will be integrated into the Form One skills development program,
    with Chinese and English teachers delivering sessions in class.
 All subjects will embed reading elements, such as including relevant materials in pre-
    learning or assessment tasks.
 The English department will sustain collaboration with QSiP to promote cross-curricular
    reading, creating interdisciplinary experiences.

Major Concern 2: To develop students’ self-learning ability

Achievements

 Based on APASO survey data in the generic skills section, 85.7% of junior students and
    84.1% of senior students demonstrated strong active learning abilities (Q-values of 116 for
    juniors and 115 for seniors), reflecting effective self-regulation in learning processes.
    Approximately 70% of teachers reported an increase in students' initiative in managing their
    studies compared to the previous year, with programs like self-directed learning workshops
    contributing to this growth.
 This academic year, cross-subject initiatives in Life & Society, Citizenship & Social
    Development, and Physical Education incorporated self-regulation elements, such as goal-
    setting exercises and reflective journals. Professional development days featured sharing
    sessions where teachers discussed integrating self-management strategies into assessments,
    like in Science and Economics.

Reflection

 While active learning scores are high, about 40% of students across levels expressed
    neutrality on their ability to independently regulate study habits without external prompts.
    Teachers identified factors like heavy academic workloads (52% for juniors, 48% for
    seniors) and lack of personalized tools as barriers. There is potential to further cultivate self-
    regulating skills by emphasizing practical applications and monitoring progress more closely
    in the next year.
 The integration of self-regulation training in the primary-to-secondary transition and
    ongoing QSiP collaborations has yielded positive results, with high Q-values indicating
    students' growing capabilities. Recommendations include expanding these efforts to include
    more peer-led activities and digital tools for self-monitoring to build greater autonomy.

Feedback and Follow-up

 Self-regulation training will be incorporated into Form One orientation and extended to all
    levels through targeted workshops on goal-setting and time management.
 Subjects will include self-regulating components in curricula, such as reflective tasks in
    assignments and self-assessment rubrics.
 The school will continue partnering with QSiP to develop cross-curricular self-regulation
    programs, fostering experiences that encourage independent learning across disciplines.

Major Concern 3: To enhance students’ sense of well-being through
                  positive education

Achievements

 The school has continued to utilize the PERMA model, a scientifically based positive
    psychology framework, to carry out various types of activities. As in the previous two years,
    Positive Education training has been provided for staff, along with professional sharing
    sessions conducted by teachers.
 Positive outcomes:
    ➢ As revealed by the APASO results: Compared to 2023–24, in terms of the psychological

      domain, both junior and senior forms showed improvement. The junior forms increased
      from 31.2 to 36.9 (P-value), while the senior forms showed a significant increase from
      40.3 to 58.1(P-value).
    ➢ Based on teachers’ observations, students have developed a stronger sense of gratitude,

      learned how to serve the community, and demonstrated a strong sense of national identity.
 Various units under the Student Affairs Committee have implemented and organized
    numerous activities based on the annual plan.
    ➢ Discipline Unit: In line with Annual Plan Objective 3.3, the unit joined the territory-wide

      School Outreach Vaccination Programme and held an anti–etomidate (“space oil”) drug
      seminar as a preventive measure. It also participated in tertiary-led health research to
      better understand students’ lifestyles for future interventions.
    ➢ Moral, Civic, and National Education Unit: The unit continued routine national and values

      education and coordinated implementation across subject panels. A new collaboration
      with external organizations to deliver values lessons was less effective, so future lessons
      may be led by class teachers with school-developed materials.
    ➢ Student Support Unit: With a more focused approach, the unit offered individualized

      subject selection guidance for SEN students in collaboration with social workers, helping
      S6 and S3 students explore strengths and career paths (Objective 3.1).
    ➢ Careers and Life Planning Unit: The unit has continued to utilized HKBM (CLAP@JC)

      to enhance the quality of CLP education. It placed greater emphasis on promoting
      internship experiences, with over 60 senior students participating in placements over the
      past two years. Through reflecting on VASK (values, attitudes, skills, and knowledge),
      students developed clearer career and study goals (Objective 3.1).
    ➢ Student Counselling Unit: The work of the unit ranged from large-scale events (e.g.,

      “Well-being School Carnival 2025”) to group and individual interventions (e.g.,
      cyberbullying workshops, arts therapy). Key focus areas included character strengths,
      emotional regulation, peer relationships, and resilience, addressing Objectives 3.1 and 3.3.
      Some large-scale activities successfully engaged multiple stakeholders, served as
      exemplary practices
    ➢ OLE Coordination Unit: Events such as the “少清校園祭” and “全方位課外活動節”

       involved the Student Union and clubs, promoting student-led organization and aligning
       with Objective 3.2.

Reflection

 Facilitating Factors:
    ➢ Dedicated Staff: The commitment and enthusiasm of our staff continue to be recognized.

       Specifically, various units within the Student Affairs Committee have worked
       collaboratively in line with the concept of positive education.
    ➢ Positive Climate: The school fosters a positive climate through staff sharing, inclusive

       activities, and the building of positive relationships.
    ➢ Supportive    Policy and Stakeholders/Resources: Senior management recognizes the
       importance of positive education and actively supports its implementation. The school
       has also collaborated closely with various parties, including social workers, NGOs,
       psychologists, and territory-wide initiatives such as CLAP@JC.
 Hindering Factors:
    ➢ More focused professional development: Basic training on PERMA-related concepts has

       been completed over the past three years. The next step is to identify the core elements of
       positive psychology and strengthen their application to maximize impact.
    ➢ Greater   whole-school approach required: Positive education should be more deeply
       integrated into school policy, management, and curriculum to ensure consistency,
       sustainability, and a genuine paradigm shift.
    ➢ Time constraints: The demanding schedule in both academic and non-academic areas, as

       shown by APASO statistics (P=85.7), leaves limited time for positive education activities,
       making it difficult to balance academic rigor with character development.

Feedback and Follow-up

 Growth mindset cultivation: During the Staff Development Day (June 2025), opinions were
    collected on the draft of the new three-year plan. Most colleagues affirmed the direction of
    positive education and suggested ways to further promote growth mindset and build student
    confidence.
 More whole-school approach to positive education: Building on the strong foundation of
    numerous ECAs and existing facilities, the school will integrate more positive education
    elements across different levels. Small-scale research will also be carried out to evaluate
    effectiveness. A Positive Education Working Group will be established to strengthen
    coordination and support.
 Dual class teacher system: This system can be further utilized through greater integration
    during class teacher periods, with an emphasis on enhancing teacher involvement.

(3) Student Performance

➢ Over the past years, subject teachers have arranged various types of learning activities for their
   students. Students have studied well and achieved excellent results in the recent public
   examinations. The most outstanding students have received offers with high admission scores
   for programs such as Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery (HKU), Bachelor of Social
   Sciences (Government and Laws) and Bachelor of Laws (Double Degree) (HKU), Risk
   Management Science (CUHK), Bachelor of Engineering/Science with an Extended Major in
   Artificial Intelligence (UST), Bachelor of Science in Occupational Therapy (PolyU), and more.

➢ Furthermore, teachers encouraged students to participate in external competitions to develop
   their diverse abilities in various activities. Students participated in academic debates and
   STEAM-related competitions. Excellent results were achieved, such as Grand Final Best
   Speaker in the HK Schools Debating Competition, Champion in the VEX Robotic Challenge,
   Gold Awards in the HK International Mathematical Olympiad (Heat Round) and Asia
   International Mathematical Olympiad Open Contest (Heat Round), and a Gold Award in the
   Electronic Circuit Construction Challenge.

➢ Students who participated in visual arts, music, and sports competitions achieved a vast number
   of awards. The newly established Handchime Team won the Champion (with Platinum Award)
   in the BELLPRINTS International Handbell Competition. A student was named the All-round
   Outstanding Athlete among NT Secondary Schools, and another was shortlisted as a Visual
   Artist in the Student of the Year Awards organized by SCMP.

➢ Participation in extracurricular activities was generally satisfactory among students, with some
   active participants achieving remarkable accomplishments, even excelling in high-standard
   competitions. They won in the Tsuen Kwan Tsing District Outstanding Students Award, Tsuen
   Kwan Tsing District Outstanding Students Election, HK Outstanding Student Award for Social
   Services, The Greater Bay Area Outstanding Students Awards, and more.

➢ The school uniform groups, including Girl Guides, St. John Ambulance Cadets, and St. John
   Nursing Cadets, play a vital role in school services. Representatives from each group raise the
   National Flag in ceremonies. Notably, a Girl Guide won the award for "Outstanding Girl
   Guide," and the cadets have been helpful on many occasions with first aid duties.

➢ Overall, our students demonstrated a positive and proactive learning attitude, with strong
   expressive abilities; however, they lacked sufficient confidence. Individual students with
   potential have displayed commendable leadership skills after participating in leadership
   training activities.
