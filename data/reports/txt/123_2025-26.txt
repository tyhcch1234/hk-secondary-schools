School Vision & Mission

Our Vision
Keeping with the tradition of Christian education, our school aims to holistically develop a whole person
who is caring, self-confident, loving, and embraces all that is good, true and beautiful. All students will
graduate as healthy individuals, both in mind and body. As our school motto goes, “I am not born for myself
alone”, all Jubileans will be nurtured to love and serve the community.


Our Mission
Bishop Hall Jubilee School was built to commemorate the Silver Jubilee of the consecration of the Rt. Rev.
Ronald Owen Hall as the (Anglican) Bishop of the Diocese of Hong Kong, Macao and South China.

The mission of the school is to make available to boys and girls a sound secondary education guided by
Christian principles as professed by Sheng Kung Hui.

We strive to exhort our students to emulate Bishop Hall, a great and godly man who dedicated his whole
life to serving the people of Hong Kong and China, in the spirit of his family motto – Non Nascor Mihi
Solum (I am not born for myself alone), which was adopted as the school motto.


School Goals
Our school values each student as a unique creation of God and is endowed with God’s gift. We aim to
create a caring and positive learning environment to achieve the following school goals:
 1. Incorporate Christian values and cultivate students’ moral and ethical consciousness to foster students’
    positive personal development.
 2. Promote a culture of active service to others as a reflection of learning about the Christian faith and
    school motto.
 3. Nurture students in the spirit of the school’s core values of trustworthiness, perseverance, love, self-
    motivation, and self-discipline to help them become servant leaders.
 4. Help students develop a global vision, appreciate the diversity of societies and cultures, and have a
    better understanding of our community and the world.
 5. Create a language-rich environment to help students communicate effectively in both Chinese and
    English.
 6. Equip students with the nine generic skills (collaborative, communicative, creative, critical thinking
    skills, information technology skills, numeracy, problem-solving skills, self-management skills, and
    study skills) to help them learn to become proactive and independent learners who are able to work
    effectively in an increasingly interdependent and fast-changing world.
 7. Encourage students to learn outside the classroom to broaden their horizons.
 8. Provide opportunities for students to explore and stretch their potential in both academic and non-
    academic development.
 9. Appreciate the beauty and qualities of aesthetic expressions of different forms.
10. Understand the need for good physical and mental health in order to lead a healthy and balanced life.

7. Review of Major Concerns

7.1 Major Concern I: To enhance students’ learning capacity and scholastic competence through
comprehensive strategies in teaching and learning

7.1.1 To nurture students’ scholastic mindset and competence

Achievements

During the 2025/2026 academic year, the school implemented a range of talent development initiatives,
STEAM learning opportunities, and mentorship schemes to stretch students' potential. From the teacher
evaluation survey, the provision of gifted programmes and academic competitions (such as Mathematics
Olympiads and debating) received a high average rating of 4.1 out of 5. Teachers also rated the Elite and
Careers Mentorship programmes at 3.87 out of 5 for providing structured, personalised guidance to targeted
students. Furthermore, hands-on STEAM workshops designed to foster a "Maker Culture" and sharpen
generic skills earned a teacher rating of 3.81 out of 5, while encouraging participation in external
competitions was rated at 3.98 out of 5.

Student survey data demonstrated positive growth, with 34.1% of students (12.4% strongly agree, 21.7%
agree; mean score 3.28/5) affirming that gifted programmes helped them develop their talents. In mentorship,
34.4% of students (13.0% strongly agree, 21.4% agree; mean score 3.31/5) reported receiving clear personal
guidance, and 40.1% (13.7% strongly agree, 26.4% agree; mean score 3.39/5) felt mentorship activities
benefited students across all ability levels. In STEAM workshops, 37.9% of students (14.6% strongly agree,
23.3% agree; mean score 3.33/5) noted improvement in generic skills such as communication, problem-
solving, and creativity. External competitions significantly broadened student perspectives, with 46.3%
feeling encouraged to join (mean score 3.48/5) and 47.8% noting that outside competitions opened their
eyes to new knowledge (mean score 3.52/5). Overall, 45.6% of students (14.9% strongly agree, 30.7% agree;
mean score 3.44/5) reported building a strong learning attitude and daily study skills.

Flagship achievements throughout the year included student participation in the Poland Model United
Nations Conference and Cultural Study Tour, the Form 4 Career Study Tour to Zhongshan and Zhuhai in
the Greater Bay Area, the World Scholar’s Cup Regional Round, and the Harvard Prize Book Award 2025
where three Form 5 students were honoured. Additionally, school teams achieved outstanding results in the
UNSDGs Debating Competition 2025/26, the Basic Law Inter-School Debate Competition, and the EMI
Drama Fest 2025, where the original production "The Price" won both the Outstanding Teamwork and
Outstanding Creativity awards.

Reflection

The evaluation data highlights a distinct perception alignment across stakeholder groups. While teachers
expressed strong confidence in the quality and depth of gifted and competition offerings (4.1/5), student


 BHJS 2025-26                                                                                           11
survey averages (ranging from 3.28/5 to 3.52/5) indicate a high neutral response percentage. Analysis
reveals that intensive gifted and STEAM opportunities have historically catered primarily to high-achieving
cohorts or specialised school teams. As a result, general students in regular classes feel less directly
impacted by these specialised programmes. Expanding talent development strategies from an elite-focused
model into mainstream classroom instruction remains a key strategic priority.

Feedback and Follow-up

To ensure that talent development reaches a wider student demographic, the school will embed tiered lift-
off modules and higher-order thinking tasks directly into regular subject curricula. STEAM education will
expand hands-on, project-based learning modules into Form 1 and Form 2, fostering an inclusive "Maker
Culture" through inter-class exhibitions and collaborative challenges. Additionally, the mentorship
framework will be enhanced by pairing senior student leaders with junior forms to deliver structured peer
guidance and early talent cultivation across all academic profiles



7.1.2 To further nurture students to become conscientious and reflective learners

Achievements

The school executed systematic measures to enhance students' self-management, executive function (EF)
skills, and future academic planning. Evaluation data indicate that teachers gave a high rating of 4.06 out of
5 to guidance activities designed to increase student awareness of further study opportunities, such as Form
3 subject selection and JUPAS choices. Teachers rated students' development of executive function skills
(goal setting, organisation, time management) at 3.44 out of 5 and school guidance on managing digital
distractions during study time at 3.52 out of 5.

Student feedback reflected positive outcomes in self-directed learning. A total of 51.6% of students (13.4%
strongly agree, 38.2% agree; mean score 3.49/5) agreed that they improved self-management skills,
including goal setting and time management. In career and elective planning, 50.6% of students (18.6%
strongly agree, 32.0% agree; mean score 3.55/5) reported a clear understanding of future study choices and
elective selection. Furthermore, 45.6% of students (15.5% strongly agree, 30.1% agree; mean score 3.43/5)
acknowledged that school guidance encouraged them to become more hardworking and reflective.
Regarding digital habits, 43.8% of students (14.6% strongly agree, 29.2% agree; mean score 3.42/5)
successfully applied school-taught strategies to reduce phone and social media distractions while studying.

Key initiatives supporting these goals included the Form 5 Careers Fair, where returning alumni from
professions such as medicine, engineering, law, and accountancy provided career insights. Additionally, the
school hosted the HKPAA Career Planning Talk for Form 4 students and organised a BAFS student visit to
CW CPA firm to explore cross-border financial services.

Reflection

Evaluation data confirms that guidance regarding future pathways and elective selection is effective and
well-received by both teachers (4.06/5) and students (3.55/5). However, student ratings for executive
function skills (3.49/5) and managing digital distractions (3.42/5) indicate that developing consistent self-
regulation habits requires continued effort. While students benefit from large-scale career talks and alumni
fairs, routine goal-setting and reflection sessions during Class Teacher Periods can sometimes feel abstract.
Increasing interactive and personalised reflection tools will help students translate advice into daily study
routines.

Feedback and Follow-up

The school will refine Class Teacher Periods by integrating practical self-management toolkits, digital usage
logs, and structured study planners into Form 1 to Form 3 routines. Form-specific workshops focusing on
time management and executive function coaching will be paired with small-group reflection circles. Career
guidance will emphasise experiential activities, including job shadowing and small-group alumni mentoring,
enabling students to connect long-term aspirations with actionable, self-directed daily learning habits.



7.1.3 To further enhance students’ language competence and cultivate their reading habit

Achievements
Strategies to cultivate a language-rich campus environment and foster sustainable reading habits yielded
strong outcomes. Teachers evaluated English immersion strategies at 3.94 out of 5 and support for language
competitions at 3.98 out of 5. Measures to enhance reading culture—including regular student book sharing,
the formal establishment of the English Book Club, and providing expanded library resources (such as e-
Newspapers and e-Magazines)—earned teacher ratings of 3.63 out of 5 and 3.67 out of 5, respectively.
From the student survey, 44.1% of respondents (15.5% strongly agree, 28.6% agree; mean score 3.46/5)
affirmed that they enjoyed practising languages through school-based language activities. In competition
engagement, 40.4% of students (15.2% strongly agree, 25.2% agree; mean score 3.39/5) felt supported and
motivated to participate. Regarding reading habits, 37.5% of students (12.7% strongly agree, 24.8% agree;
mean score 3.30/5) reported building regular reading routines, while the provision of diverse library reading
materials was rated at 3.33 out of 5.
Students demonstrated language proficiency through various external accolades. Achievements included
strong showings in the World Scholar’s Cup, three Form 5 students winning the Harvard Prize Book Award
2025, active participation in the UNSDGs Debating Competition 2025/26 and Basic Law Inter-School
Debate Competition, and awards in the EMI Drama Fest 2025.


Reflection

The data demonstrates high effectiveness in creating authentic English learning environments and
supporting elite language teams. However, internalising daily reading habits across the general student
population remains an area for continued growth, as reflected in student reading habit ratings (3.30/5) and

assemblies and campus book fairs. Furthermore, authentic language production opportunities—such as
public speaking, debating, drama, and the English Channel—will be expanded to engage a broader student
audience.



7.1.4 To nurture the digital and information literacy of our students

Achievements

The school updated its technology curriculum and refined mobile device management to prepare students
for an increasingly digital educational environment. Teachers rated the fine-tuned Computer Literacy (CL)
curriculum—which emphasises digital citizenship and Artificial Intelligence (AI) education—at 3.67 out
of 5. Compliance with and understanding of the Mobile Device Management (MDM) system on campus
received a teacher rating of 3.29 out of 5.
Student survey data indicated strong digital self-efficacy. A total of 50.9% of students (17.7% strongly agree,
33.2% agree; mean score 3.57/5) affirmed that they learned to use digital devices and the internet wisely,
safely, and correctly, particularly regarding AI tools. On device policy awareness, 45.3% of students (16.1%
strongly agree, 29.2% agree; mean score 3.39/5) understood the purpose of the MDM system, and 48.8%
(20.5% strongly agree, 28.3% agree; mean score 3.54/5) reported that following campus device rules was
straightforward.
Key achievements in digital learning included student showcases at the 2025 Digital Entertainment
Leadership Forum (DELF) featuring AI creations. Additionally, Interact Club members achieved distinction
in the HK-Macau Inter-school AI Putonghua Entrepreneur Competition.
Reflection
The integration of AI modules and digital citizenship into the junior Computer Literacy curriculum has
successfully enhanced student digital literacy and AI awareness (3.57/5). Nevertheless, the lower teacher
score regarding MDM rule compliance (3.29/5) highlights ongoing management challenges regarding off-
task digital distractions during self-directed study sessions. While students understand the technical rules of
BYOD, developing internal digital self-discipline requires continuous guidance alongside technical
management.

Feedback and Follow-up
The school will optimise BYOD management by refining MDM policy deployment and exploring screen-
time monitoring tools to help students manage digital habits. The Form 1 to Form 3 CL curriculum will
further incorporate practical modules on AI ethics, prompt engineering, and information verification. Peer
sharing sessions will be established during Class Teacher Periods to allow students to share effective
techniques for maintaining focus and utilising digital devices constructively for academic inquiry.



7.1.5 To continuously improve the effectiveness of teaching and learning (T&L)

Achievements

Pedagogical enhancement was driven through systematic assessment data analysis, e-learning integration,
and collaborative teaching practices. Teachers gave outstanding evaluations to data-driven T&L review
(4.12/5) and the effective utilisation of Learning Management Systems (e.g., Google Classroom) and
interactive e-learning tools (Padlet, Mentimeter, Kahoot!) (4.04/5). Interdisciplinary collaboration—such as
Academic Weeks and project-based learning—received a teacher rating of 3.96 out of 5. Flexible
deployment of IT and AI tools was rated at 3.77 out of 5, while the enhanced mentorship system for new
teachers earned 3.79 out of 5.
Student feedback highlighted high classroom engagement. A total of 56.5% of students (21.7% strongly
agree, 34.8% agree; mean score 3.67/5) noted that lessons were more active and engaging due to e-learning
tools. Furthermore, 55.9% of students (20.5% strongly agree, 35.4% agree; mean score 3.66/5) reported
using AI tools constructively in daily learning. In interdisciplinary learning, 47.9% of students (16.8%
strongly agree, 31.1% agree; mean score 3.50/5) affirmed that cross-subject projects enriched their learning
experience.
Core implementation milestones included Form 1 PSHE project-based learning, PSHE Academic Week,
the execution of the school's 3-Year AI Development Plan, institutional subscriptions to AI platforms for
teachers (e.g., Google Gemini), and receiving the Excellence in AI and Innovative Technology Education
Teaching Award.
Reflection
Data analysis shows that embedding assessment data (HKDSE statistical reports, TSA data, value-added
scores) into subject panel evaluations has established a structured cycle of continuous pedagogical
improvement (4.12/5). E-learning and AI tool adoption among both teachers and students is high (4.04/5
teacher rating, 3.66/5 student rating). Reflection indicates that to further elevate teaching effectiveness,
interdisciplinary collaboration should be deepened beyond standalone academic weeks into regular cross-
curricular units.
Feedback and Follow-up
The school will advance its 3-Year AI Development Plan by providing specialised professional
development on generative AI pedagogy and data-informed instruction. Cross-panel collaboration will be
strengthened by formalising joint lesson preparation and interdisciplinary project-based learning across
Form 1 PSHE and Form 2 STEAM curricula. The mentorship system for new teachers will be continuously
refined to foster professional exchange and maintain high teaching standards across all departments.

7.1.6 To enhance the academic atmosphere on the campus

Achievements

The school implemented several organisational and promotional strategies to reinforce an encouraging
academic atmosphere. Teachers rated the policy of setting a maximum quota for club and team enrollment
at 3.96 out of 5 for effectively helping students balance academic workload and extracurricular activities.
The practice of rearranging after-school team training schedules to eliminate time clashes with
supplementary lessons was rated at 3.88 out of 5. Prominent campus display boards and notice showcases
received a teacher rating of 3.85 out of 5 for fostering academic appreciation, while peer study sharing
sessions received 3.77 out of 5.
Student survey data showed positive engagement with campus learning culture. A total of 44.4% of students
(16.1% strongly agree, 28.3% agree; mean score 3.46/5) felt motivated by campus display boards
celebrating academic achievements. Peer sharing during assemblies and lessons motivated 40.0% of
students (12.4% strongly agree, 27.6% agree; mean score 3.35/5). Furthermore, 38.2% of students (12.7%
strongly agree, 25.5% agree; mean score 3.26/5) confirmed that practice schedule adjustments successfully
resolved time clashes with supplementary lessons.
Key implementation actions featured the official launch of the Positive Learning Attitude Award Scheme,
First Class Honours recognition ceremonies (celebrating Form 2 students achieving an average term mark
above 80), and visible campus displays showcasing student academic work.
Reflection
Organisational adjustments to balance academic study and extracurricular involvement have yielded
positive results, supported by strong teacher agreement (3.96/5). However, stakeholder evaluation metrics
regarding school life satisfaction and belongingness highlight the importance of recognising student
progress across all academic levels, rather than focusing solely on top-tier academic awards. Fostering an
encouraging environment where incremental effort and positive learning attitudes are celebrated is essential
for maintaining student motivation.
Feedback and Follow-up
The school will expand the Positive Learning Attitude Award Scheme to recognise non-academic
milestones, progress improvements, and consistent learning habits across all form levels. The frequency of
recognition ceremonies will be increased, supplemented by digital and physical showcases of diverse
student work. The school administration will continue to monitor tutorial and extracurricular practice
timetables dynamically to safeguard student balance, well-being, and academic focus

7.2 Major Concern II: To instil positive values and life-long aspiration into students and nurture
them to become future leaders with a national and global mindset

7.2.1 To nurture students to develop a lifelong vision of career planning

Achievements

The Career and Life Planning Committee provided timely information on further studies, individual
guidance and experience-sharing opportunities at different stages of students’ secondary school life. Various
career talks and parents’ nights were tailored for students at different levels, providing updated information
on elective subject choices and JUPAS programmes. The F.5 Career Fair, supported by alumni sharing,
offered students practical insights into JUPAS programme selection. In addition, external resources such as
the BHJS JUPAS Fair, the F.3–F.5 Tertiary Institution Fair and the HKPAA Career Planning Talk were
utilised to broaden students’ horizons in career planning. Firm visits further increased students’ exposure to
the workplace, while the F.4 Career Study Tour in the Greater Bay Area and the PTA AGM Guest Talk
helped students develop greater awareness of the wide range of job opportunities in the Mainland.

The related data showed that 50.6% of students agreed or strongly agreed that they clearly understood their
future study or elective subject choices, while 34.4% felt that they had received clear and personalised
support from elite or career mentorship programmes. Teachers also rated “Life Planning” as a learning goal
with a mean score of 4.0. In addition, the KPM 17.5 score for Meaning in Life was 115, which was higher
than the score of 97 recorded in 2025–2026. These figures suggest that the school’s career-related initiatives
helped students build a foundation for life planning, although continued support is needed to strengthen
personalised guidance.

Reflection

Although students received a wide range of information through the activities, opportunities for guided self-
reflection remained limited. Students in F.3 and F.6, who faced immediate decisions about further studies,
tended to show stronger motivation, while students at other levels appeared less proactive in planning their
future pathways. It was also observed that some senior form students were not always clear about their own
academic progress.

Feedback and Follow-up

To strengthen students’ career planning process, self-reflection tasks should be added after career-related
activities, and post-activity debriefing should be reinforced. In order to help students better understand their
progress and adopt a more proactive approach to their studies, predicted grades should be communicated
promptly after the pre-mock examinations. Timely notification would enable students to assess their
performance more accurately and make informed decisions about their academic pathways.

Achievements

A range of initiatives were implemented to enhance students’ mental and physical well-being. Fri-yay

Spiritual support also played an important role in students’ whole-person development. In addition to
regular religious assemblies, Gospel Month and festive worship services, morning prayers were arranged
on non-assembly days to encourage students to begin the day with faith, trust and care for the school and
the community. Through social service, joint-school gospel camps and themed fellowship gatherings,
students were able to understand and practise faith from different perspectives, thereby strengthening their
resilience and vitality when facing challenges in life.

Student support activities were also well received. Activities such as Forest Bathing, the City Discovery
Bus Tour, Floral Art Class, Organic Farm Visit, Baking Workshop, Popcorn Giveaway Day, Gelato
Giveaway and Student Support Day, Fortune Cookies Giveaway Day, and the Dubai Chocolate Pistachio
Mochi Workshop provided students with enjoyable and relaxing experiences. Some students developed
strong rapport and trust with school social workers, which encouraged their active participation in Guidance
Team activities. Emotion Education lessons during Class Teacher Periods were engaging, and students
participated enthusiastically in activities related to breathing techniques and accepting both positive and
negative emotions. Class teachers observed that students generally enjoyed positive peer relationships, and
they shared class-based practices such as class lunches and informal gatherings to strengthen rapport and
provide opportunities for students to express themselves or raise concerns. It is suggested that class teachers
continue to organise such informal gatherings for rapport building.

The quantitative data further reflected the positive impact of these initiatives on students’ well-being. Half
of the students agreed or strongly agreed that Guidance Committee activities helped them feel happy and
relaxed, and 56.2% of students who used the Wellness Zone considered it helpful for relaxation. In terms
of physical health, 73.0% of F1 boys and 86.8% of F1 girls were within the acceptable weight range, while
84.7% of F1 boys and 77.4% of F1 girls attained at least bronze level in the School Physical Fitness Award
Scheme. Students’ emotional state also showed improvement, with the Q-scores for Positive Affect, No
Anxiety and Depressive Symptoms, and No Negative Affect reaching 97, 100 and 105 respectively, all
higher than last year. The scores for School Atmosphere, including Not Lonely and Belongingness, were
105 and 111, respectively, and the KPM score for Physical Exercise was 95, also higher than last year.

Reflection

Regarding Emotion Education lessons, teachers observed that although students gained useful knowledge,
they found it harder to demonstrate their understanding through concrete actions. Teachers also observed a



 BHJS 2025-26                                                                                              18
polarisation in stress levels, with some academically focused students experiencing considerable pressure
while others remained overly relaxed about upcoming examinations. In the coming year, the school should
explore a wider variety of themes and provide more values education to help students build healthy
interpersonal relationships. Teachers also noted that delivering class teacher materials could be challenging
for Non-Chinese Speaking students when the materials were mainly provided in Chinese; this issue should
therefore be further reviewed.

In terms of physical well-being, students should be further guided to develop healthy eating habits and
maintain a balanced diet. The arrangement for junior form class teachers to have lunch with students at the
beginning of the school year should be continued, as it helps strengthen teacher-student interaction. At the
same time, the school should formally establish clear expectations and rules before the first lunchtime of
the school year.

Participation in morning sports competitions also required further attention, as athletically stronger students
often dominated inter-class games while others remained as spectators. Teachers expressed a wish to involve
more students and provide broader participation opportunities in future competitions. At the same time, the
school should promote positive practices: many male students actively participated in the Sports Society,
and students gradually developed a habit of regular exercise by independently completing the physical
exercises required by the PE Department’s “Daily AI Monitor”. Some students also used iPads to record
and reflect on their body postures during PE lessons. Peer support strategies may be introduced to encourage
wider participation in physical fitness activities, and more form-based sports activities, such as obstacle
races, could be organised to increase engagement.

Feedback and Follow-up

To enhance students’ well-being, particularly among F5 students, it was recommended that peer
communication platforms, such as “chit-chat” activities and dedicated events, be launched to facilitate peer
conversations and give students opportunities to voice their concerns. Physical relaxation groups could also
be organised, combining relaxation sessions with structured group conversations so students have a safe,
open space to express their worries.



7.2.3 To cultivate positive attitudes and values in students and foster students’ sense of national
identity

Achievements

A variety of learning opportunities helped cultivate students’ positive attitudes, values and sense of national
identity. Teachers stated that students found the insights, experiences and success stories shared by alumni
in assemblies and career fairs particularly interesting and helpful. Service learning activities, including the
Interact Club Korea Service Trip, CU Christmas Carolling, F.3 Mary Rose Service Scheme, Wellness and
Joy Volunteer Service, voluntary service at Treasure Life, Be Kind to Animals Association activities and
Food Distribution Social Service, provided students with meaningful opportunities to serve others. The
Thrive and Shine Programme was also enhanced with newly added elements on national education and
media literacy. Many students, especially junior form students, actively participated in exchange activities
with the sister school in Guangzhou, while students also took part enthusiastically in the St. Catharine’s


 BHJS 2025-26                                                                                              19
School for Girls Exchange Programme organised by the Student Union, with most demonstrating a strong
sense of responsibility in hosting visiting students.

Flag-raising training expanded, and MCNE Ambassadors delivered speeches under the National Flag
instead of teachers. Students’ sharing was more relatable and inspiring, helping the whole school better
understand the country’s history and strengthen their sense of national identity. Activities such as the
National Security Exhibition Visit, Hong Kong Museum of History Visit, livestreaming of the National Day
Military Parade, and the Commemorative Morning Assembly and Exhibition for the Victory of the War of
Resistance against Japan enhanced students’ understanding of national history and awareness of national
security. Chinese Culture Week also enabled students to experience the beauty of Chinese culture through
engaging activities.

The relevant data indicated encouraging progress in students’ values education and national identity. 48.4%
of students felt that Class Teacher Periods helped them understand the country’s history and development,
and 56.2% felt that these periods helped them identify as Chinese citizens. Students achieved a Q-score of
116 and a P-score of 85.7 across all National Identity subscales, including responsibility, pride, and respect
for the national flag and anthem. Junior students’ participation in National Education competitions reached
70.0%. The KPM scores for Act of Honesty and Importance of Morality were both 116, higher than last
year. Participation in sister school exchange programmes and Mainland exchange programmes also
increased, with KPM 8.3.1 rising from 0.15 in 2024–2025 to 0.74 in 2025–2026, and KPM 8.3.3 rising from
0.11 to 0.57 over the same period.

Reflection

New uniformed groups, including the Sea Cadets and Red Cross, were introduced, but students’ acceptance
of uniformed teams still needs improvement, and further methods to attract participation should be explored.
Respect also emerged as a core theme for reflection. Students developed a habit of criticising one another
during competitions instead of offering verbal support, and class teachers noted that students sometimes
showed a general lack of respect for personal boundaries. Moreover, students without Christian
backgrounds or values sometimes showed limited respect during morning prayers and announcements. It is
essential to teach students appropriate behaviour and ways to show respect for other cultures and beliefs.
Regarding national identity, teachers observed that students had a strong sense of national identity, but
suggested that some materials, such as those related to developments in China’s flight industry, should be
simplified or better adapted for Form 1 students.

Feedback and Follow-up

In the coming year, promotion of uniformed groups should be strengthened. Newly established uniformed
groups should also be given more opportunities to serve the school community so as to increase their
visibility and build a positive image among students.

The school should further promote respect and empathy. These values may be integrated with Chinese
cultural promotion activities to help students cultivate traditional Chinese virtues. Through the practice of
propriety, students should learn to participate in religious rituals with respect and to appreciate different
cultures with an attitude of harmony in diversity.

The national education materials used in Class Teacher Periods should be further refined. In addition to
being better aligned with students’ ability levels, the materials should also cater to the learning needs of
Non-Chinese Speaking students.

7.2.4 To cultivate students’ national and global mindsets

Achievements

The Form 4 Career Study Tour in the Greater Bay Area provided valuable opportunities for students to
broaden their horizons, deepen their understanding of the country’s development and experience its rich
cultural heritage firsthand. A wide range of global learning activities, including the World Scholar’s Cup,
Poland Model United Nations Conference and Cultural Study Tour, an exchange programme with Koshi
High School, the UNSDGs Debating Competition, and a choir exchange visit with the University of
Southern California, provided diverse exchange opportunities for students. These activities enabled students
to experience different cultures and cultivate an international perspective through both overseas experiences
and local activities. Assembly sharing by Environmental Ambassadors, MCNE Ambassadors, the Interact
Club, and others also helped students gain a deeper understanding of topics such as sustainable development,
cultural integration, and national development.

The data also showed that students were given meaningful exposure to national and global learning
experiences. Among S6 students, 48.0% had personally participated in at least one school-arranged
Mainland exchange or learning activity during their secondary education. Teachers also rated “National and
Global Identity” with a mean score of 4.1, suggesting that the school’s exchange opportunities and related
learning activities were generally recognised as supporting students’ development of national and global
mindsets.

Reflection

To further cultivate students’ national mindset, education on the Constitution and the Basic Law could be
strengthened. This would help students understand the relevance of these laws to their daily lives, thereby
enhancing their national awareness and respect for the rule of law.

To further cultivate students’ global perspectives, elements related to sustainable development currently
embedded in different subject curricula and morning assembly sharing could be better consolidated. This
would help students develop a deeper understanding of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals
and foster their concern for global development as well as their sense of responsibility as global citizens.

Feedback and Follow-up

Committees may take a more proactive role in exploring external resources to arrange richer, more diverse
law-related learning activities for students. In addition to mock trial activities and Model United Nations
competitions, students could also participate in programmes organised by professional bodies such as the
Law Society. The promotion of the Sustainable Development Goals may begin with campus displays, while
morning assemblies, weekly assemblies and Campus TV should be further utilised to enhance students’
understanding of related issues.

7.2.5 To boost students’ abilities and mindsets to become future leaders

Achievements

Activities such as the joint-school exchange programme organised by the Student Union, the CCA Fair held
by 36 societies and clubs, Chinese Culture Week, STEM Week, PSHE Week and Guidance Days enabled
students to develop their leadership, planning, collaboration and problem-solving skills. Through feedback
from these activities, students could also reflect on their strengths and weaknesses and identify ways to
enhance their leadership qualities.

Supporting data suggested that the school provided a reasonable platform for students to develop leadership
and service-related experiences. Teachers rated the statement that the school provides adequate
opportunities for students to develop leadership abilities with a mean score of 4.0. In addition, 36.8% of
junior students participated in uniformed groups or community service, reflecting students’ initial
involvement in service and leadership-related activities.

Reflection

It was observed that F.2 students were generally less willing to take up leadership roles, particularly high-
achieving students who tended to prioritise their academic studies. It is suggested that student ambassadors
be trained for major school events, such as summer concert receptions, to enhance students’ sense of
belonging and responsibility in serving the school. Teachers are also encouraged to reinforce the roles and
responsibilities of class committees to cultivate students’ sense of accountability; for example, reinforcing
classroom rules may help encourage students to take greater ownership of their responsibilities. Student
leaders also frequently felt disheartened because peers and the school community often criticised or
commented negatively on how they carried out school activities. In addition, there was a clear lack of formal
leadership training customised for junior forms. The school should deliberately support lower-achieving
students and pay close attention to training passive students, given that only 40.1% currently felt that their
leadership mindset was being developed.

Feedback and Follow-up

Leadership training initiatives should be strategically extended to the lower forms to nurture student leaders
at an earlier stage. A new leadership training programme for junior form students will be introduced, and
post-training support for society and committee leaders should be further strengthened. To counter peer
discouragement, the school should provide formal acknowledgement, appreciation and motivation to affirm
student leaders’ efforts and sustain their commitment to serving the school community.
