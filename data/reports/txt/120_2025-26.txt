      Major Concern 1: To leverage AI to customize student learning experience

 Achievements
    In 2025–2026, the school further extended the use of artificial intelligence across different Key
    Learning Areas and student development programmes. AI applications moved beyond general
    information searching and were increasingly incorporated into teaching, assessment, feedback,
    creative work, project learning and self-directed learning.

    Subject panels adopted AI in ways suited to their respective disciplines. In English Language, an AI-
    powered writing platform was used across all forms to provide students with immediate feedback
    on writing. Although discrepancies between AI-generated and teacher-generated marks were
    observed, many students found the instant feedback and post-writing exercises useful. In Chinese
    Language, teachers across different forms incorporated AI-generated texts, images and videos into
    language activities, while senior-form students experienced AI-assisted composition feedback. In
    Chemistry, an AI bot was developed and an AI-supported homework task was piloted with F.3
    students to support self-learning.

    AI was also used to support more personalised and inquiry-based learning. In Civic Education,
    students combined AI-generated descriptions with their own observations during a local cultural
    study, selected areas of interest for further exploration and critically identified inaccuracies in AI-
    generated information before incorporating personal reflections into their writing. Biology
    integrated AI into adaptive practice, diagnostic feedback and virtual laboratory simulations, with
    teachers using learning data to identify gaps and adjust instruction. History used AI-generated
    learning samples, bilingual materials and project support, with over 70% of participating students in
    several groups reporting that these resources supported their learning.

    AI also broadened opportunities for creativity and applied learning. About 90% of F.4 Music students
    completed creative music projects using AI-assisted tools, while Visual Arts students experimented
    with generative AI for idea development in design work. In Physical Education and related cross-
    curricular activities, students experimented with video and AI analysis to review sports performance.

    Attention was also given to students with different learning needs. The Student Support Team
    piloted AI-assisted tiered assignments in Citizenship and Social Development. Although
    implementation was more limited than originally planned, students' work indicated that lower-
    achieving students were able to produce more complete and better-structured responses.

    Teacher capacity and school-wide support for AI education were strengthened. Junior Science
    teachers participated in professional training conducted with the HKU AI in Education Lab; 80%
    submitted AI-integrated lesson plans and collegial lesson-sharing was conducted. The IT Section also
    developed a school-wide AI governance framework and provided programmes on AI literacy, ethics
    and responsible use. The STEAM Education Committee also supported whole-school professional
    development, with all teachers participating in AI-related training, including a staff development
    programme on space science and AI.
Reflection
The year's work demonstrated a clear broadening of AI integration across the curriculum.
Teachers were increasingly able to identify subject-specific applications instead of treating AI
as a stand-alone technology. Students experienced AI as a tool for feedback, revision,
creativity, inquiry and self-directed learning.

More importantly, several panels began to move from merely asking students to use AI
towards teaching them how to evaluate and challenge AI output. This represents an important
development in AI literacy. In Civic Education, students compared AI-generated descriptions
against first-hand observation; in Geography, students were reminded to verify AI-generated
information against reliable sources; and Computer Studies reinforced the importance of
independent reasoning instead of directly using AI to solve coding problems.

Nevertheless, the depth of implementation remained uneven. In some areas, AI genuinely
supported differentiation or personalised feedback, while in others its application remained
at the level of content generation, information searching or experimental trials. Some planned
initiatives could not be fully implemented because of lesson-time constraints, technical
limitations or uncertainty about suitable pedagogical approaches.

Reliability and over-reliance also emerged as significant concerns. English teachers observed
inconsistencies in AI-generated writing scores, Physics found that AI-generated examination
questions did not consistently meet HKEAA conventions, and Geography reported difficulties
among students in verifying information and transforming AI-generated content into their own
coherent writing. Ethics and Religious Studies similarly observed that some students relied
excessively on AI when completing assignments.

The experience of this year therefore suggests that the next stage of development should focus
less on increasing the number of AI activities and more on improving their pedagogical quality
and their actual contribution to customised learning.
Feedback and Follow-up
To build on the progress made, we can:

•   Further develop AI-enhanced learning strategies that provide differentiated tasks, diagnostic
    feedback and appropriate learning pathways for students with different abilities and needs.
•   Strengthen students' AI literacy, particularly their ability to verify information, identify
    inaccuracies and bias, protect privacy, use AI ethically and retain independent judgement.
•   Encourage panels to move beyond AI-assisted content generation towards applications that
    demonstrably improve feedback, assessment, self-directed learning and learning outcomes.
•   Continue professional development and collegial sharing so that effective AI practices can be
    transferred across subjects, while maintaining appropriate teacher moderation and
    professional judgement.
•   Develop clearer school-wide mechanisms for evaluating the effectiveness of AI-assisted
    learning, with greater use of student work, learning progress and feedback rather than relying
    solely on participation figures.
     Major concern 2: To live up to the characteristics of excellent Maryknollers: 4Rs

    Achievements
    Throughout the year, the school promoted the 4Rs through curriculum learning, personal
    growth education, discipline programmes, leadership development, service learning and
    everyday school routines. The emphasis was placed not only on students' understanding of the
    values but increasingly on translating them into observable behaviour.

    Respect was reinforced in different contexts. Teachers' Day activities encouraged students to
    express gratitude to teachers, while Guidance programmes such as the KTMC Fuel Station,
    HeArtFul Days and peer-support activities promoted mutual care and positive relationships.
    Discipline assemblies addressed verbal bullying and the use of hurtful nicknames, while
    subject-based activities reinforced respect for classmates, teachers, cultural diversity,
    performers, rules and shared facilities.

    Responsibility and doing the right thing at the right time were incorporated into daily learning
    habits. Panels emphasised punctual submission of assignments, preparation for lessons, care
    of equipment, teamwork and appropriate conduct. Biology reported improvements in
    collaboration, accountability and punctuality through project and group work, while Music
    used study-attitude assessment, rehearsal discipline and concert etiquette to reinforce
    responsible behaviour.

    Personal Growth Lessons and the Reflection Journal provided a whole-school framework for
    values education. Teachers observed that students made genuine efforts to reflect on form-
    specific values, and goal-setting helped students consider personal responsibility and self-
    management.

    Service and leadership provided particularly authentic opportunities for students to practise
    the 4Rs. Civic Education incorporated explicit reflection on the 4Rs into community service.
    Students demonstrated responsibility through preparation and punctuality, respect through
    appropriate communication with different service recipients, and sound judgement by
    adapting their conduct to different situations.

    Student leadership was also strengthened. Thirty new prefects were recruited and overall
    prefect duty attendance exceeded 75%. Inclusive Ambassadors recorded strong engagement
    and willingly undertook follow-up duties, while student leaders, peer mediators, Big Brothers,
    IT Ambassadors and other student groups served their peers and the school in different capacities.
    Reflection
    The 4Rs became increasingly visible across different dimensions of school life. The use of
    service learning, leadership duties, personal reflection and classroom routines helped turn
    abstract values into concrete actions. Evidence from students' service reflections was
    particularly encouraging, showing that many students could relate responsibility, respect
    and appropriate decision-making to authentic situations rather than simply repeat the
    language of the 4Rs.
The whole-school approach also enabled the same values to be reinforced in different
settings. Students encountered the 4Rs through formal lessons, assemblies, activities,
sports, music, project work, service, leadership and pastoral programmes. This consistency
helped establish a common language for student development.

However, the extent to which the 4Rs became consistent everyday habits remained
uneven. While 54% of F.1–F.5 students earned a merit during the year and many students
demonstrated positive conduct, the number of late arrivals increased by 17% compared
with 2024–2025. School Affairs also reported only slight improvement in punctual
submission of documents and noted that some students continued to require reminders
regarding appropriate behaviour during flag-raising and the national anthem.

Some panel reports similarly indicated that good behaviour was stronger in structured
activities than in everyday situations. This suggests that students may understand the
intended values but do not yet consistently internalise them in routine school life.
The next stage should therefore focus less on introducing additional activities under the
4Rs and more on strengthening consistency between students' understanding, reflection
and day-to-day behaviour.
Feedback and Follow-up
To build on the progress made, we can:

•   Continue using the 4Rs as a common language for values education across lessons,
    assemblies, homeroom activities, discipline, guidance, leadership and service.
•   Place greater emphasis on observable everyday behaviours, particularly punctuality,
    responsible completion of work, respectful communication, appropriate use of electronic
    devices and adherence to school routines.
•   Strengthen reflection after service, leadership and experiential activities so that students
    can explicitly connect their actions and decisions with the 4Rs.
•   Provide more opportunities for a wider range of students, including junior-form students,
    to take on meaningful leadership and service responsibilities.
•   Develop clearer indicators for evaluating the impact of 4R education, using behavioural
    and discipline data together with student reflection and teacher observation to monitor
    progress over time.
     Major concern 3: To celebrate the school's 55th anniversary by promoting the spirit of the
      Maryknoll Fathers: faith, service and resilience

    Achievements
    The school's 55th Anniversary provided an important opportunity to reconnect the school
    community with its history and the spirit of the Maryknoll Fathers. Throughout the year, faith,
    service and resilience were promoted through religious formation, curriculum learning, community
    service, student leadership and anniversary activities.

    Faith formation remained central. Ethics and Religious Studies incorporated prayer, meditation,
    examen, pilgrimage, church visits, service learning and reflection across different forms. Students
    participated in activities such as church cleaning, charity service, pilgrimage and religious visits,
    through which they developed greater understanding of faith, gratitude, responsibility and respect.
    Parish-school collaboration also provided opportunities for students to deepen their faith through
    service.

    Faith was reinforced beyond Religious Education. Music lessons connected the school song,
    Scripture and hymns with the themes of faith, service and resilience. Hymn singing formed part of
    music learning, and the choir served at major school services and functions. Bible verses were
    incorporated into anniversary souvenirs, while major school events were used where appropriate
    to communicate the Maryknoll spirit. The Library displayed materials on the school's founder and
    publications of Maryknoll Fathers during Open Day, enabling students and visitors to learn more
    about the school's heritage.

    Service was another prominent feature. Students participated in service for elderly people, persons
    with hearing impairment, environmental causes and charitable organisations. Civic Education
    activities required students to reflect on their conduct and the values underlying their service.
    Inclusive Ambassadors, Peer Mediators, Big Brothers, prefects, IT Ambassadors, student helpers
    and other student groups also served the school community in different capacities.

    Resilience was cultivated through pastoral support and challenging learning experiences.
    Professional psychological and counselling services were positively evaluated by participating
    students, while Mental Health Week promoted coping, mutual support and class unity. Outdoor
    and co-curricular experiences also encouraged perseverance. Biology students demonstrated
    resilience and teamwork during field studies, while sustained robotics and competition training
    provided opportunities for students to develop perseverance and commitment.

    The anniversary also strengthened school identity and community connection. The 55th
    Anniversary Open Day involved more than 25 booths prepared by sections, committees and
    subject panels and attracted around 775 visitors from 190 families. The Homecoming Concert,
    Sports Fiesta, Open Day activities and other anniversary programmes offered students
    opportunities to contribute to and participate in the celebration of the school's heritage.
Reflection
The 55th Anniversary was more than a series of commemorative events. It created a platform
through which the school could revisit its Catholic and Maryknoll heritage and connect this legacy
with students' present-day experience.

Among the three dimensions, faith and service were particularly well embedded. Faith was
addressed through curriculum, prayer, liturgical life, pilgrimage, music and school events, while
service was supported by a wide range of community and school-based opportunities. These
experiences enabled students to encounter the Maryknoll spirit through action rather than only
through historical knowledge.

Resilience was also promoted, although the evidence was more dispersed. Rather than being
confined to anniversary activities, resilience was developed through counselling and mental-
health support, outdoor education, sports, competitions, leadership training and sustained
participation in teams. This suggests that resilience was understood as a lived disposition
developed through challenge and perseverance.

The anniversary also provided opportunities for students, parents, alumni and the wider
community to reconnect with the school. However, the educational impact of individual
anniversary events was not always evaluated explicitly. Participation figures demonstrate reach,
but do not by themselves indicate how deeply students understood the history, mission and
values of the Maryknoll Fathers.

Future work should therefore build on the anniversary by ensuring that faith, service and
resilience remain part of the school's ongoing identity rather than being associated only with a
commemorative year.
Feedback and Follow-up
To build on the progress made, we can:

•   Sustain the Maryknoll spirit beyond the 55th Anniversary by continuing to integrate faith,
    service and resilience into curriculum, student development and major school activities.
•   Deepen students' understanding of the history, mission and contribution of the Maryknoll
    Fathers, connecting school heritage with contemporary student life.
•   Continue to provide authentic service opportunities and strengthen structured reflection
    so that students can recognise the values developed through service.
•   Further develop resilience through pastoral support, peer networks, leadership, sports,
    experiential learning and opportunities for students to face manageable challenges and
    learn from setbacks.
•   Preserve and make effective use of the legacy of the 55th Anniversary, including
    anniversary materials, student work, historical resources and alumni connections, as
    resources for future values and heritage education.

A. Students’ Attitude and Behaviour
    Engagement      Students generally demonstrated positive engagement in learning and
                    school life. Many participated actively in classroom learning, project work,
                    digital and AI-assisted learning, co-curricular activities and school events.
                    Their willingness to explore new approaches and take part in a wide range
                    of learning experiences reflected an open and proactive attitude towards
                    learning.
    Respect         Students were generally courteous and respectful towards teachers,
                    schoolmates and others in the community. Through classroom learning,
                    assemblies, service activities and student development programmes,
                    students were encouraged to show consideration for others, appreciate
                    different perspectives and build positive relationships. The promotion of
                    the 4Rs further reinforced respect as an important quality of a
                    Maryknoller.
    Responsibility Many students demonstrated a growing sense of responsibility in both
                    learning and school life. They took ownership of assigned tasks,
                    participated in class and school duties, and served as prefects, student
                    leaders, peer helpers, ambassadors and members of various service
                    teams. Students were also increasingly encouraged to use digital tools and
                    AI responsibly and exercise sound judgement in their learning.
    Collaboration Collaboration continued to be a strength among students. Through group
                    projects, performances, sports, competitions, leadership training and
                    community service, students learned to communicate, share
                    responsibilities and work towards common goals. Their participation in the
                    school's 55th Anniversary activities also provided valuable opportunities to
                    work together and contribute to the wider school community.
    Self-discipline Most students demonstrated appropriate self-management in their
                    studies and daily school routines. They were encouraged to manage their
                    time, complete assigned work, observe school expectations and make
                    responsible choices. The emphasis on doing the right thing at the right
                    time helped students connect self-discipline with the qualities expected of
                    excellent Maryknollers.

                    willingness to learn from experience. Opportunities in service, leadership,

                    belonging to the school.
